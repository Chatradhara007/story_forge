The city of Varrow was built on canals, and on the eve of the autumn Lantern Festival every window along them glowed with coloured glass. Most of that glass came from the workshop of Orla Venn on Tallow Street.

Orla had been born in Varrow and had never once left it. She was thirty-four, broad in the shoulders, and left-handed, which the other glassmakers said was why her pieces curved the wrong way and sold for twice the price. A pale scar ran across her right palm, the mark of a blowpipe that had slipped long ago. Her workshop was small and hot, built around its single furnace, which had not gone cold in eleven years.

Her apprentice was a thin, red-haired boy of sixteen called Rook. He had lost two fingers of his left hand to that furnace in his first winter, and he worked the glass with the three that remained faster than most men worked with five.

Upstairs lived old Ysolde, the last glass-singer in Varrow, who could hum a crack out of a windowpane. Orla's younger sister, Rose Halloway, kept an herb shop on Wicker Lane and came by most evenings with bread and complaints.

That evening Captain Aldous Crane of the city watch ducked in through the door. He was a tall, stooping man who seemed to apologise to every ceiling. "Someone set two fires on the east bank last week," he said. "Keep your ledger somewhere safe." The ledger was a fat book bound in green leather, the only record of every debt owed to the workshop.

On the bench, wrapped in felt, lay the Sunstone lens: a disc of gold glass as wide as a dinner plate, made for the Festival and promised to the Duke. Tom Fenner, the ferryman, had brought the sand for it across the canal himself.
