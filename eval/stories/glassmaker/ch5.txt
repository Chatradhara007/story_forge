A week later, Captain Crane came to the workshop with a warrant.

The short captain had to stand on his toes to see over the crowd of customers. When they had gone, he laid a paper on the bench. "We know who set the fires," he said. "The Marsh family's creditors. They wanted the boy frightened back home."

Tom handed the captain the warrant for the arrest. He had been the captain's clerk for four years now and was still the only one in the watch who could spell.

Crane opened Orla's green ledger, which lay on the counter, and ran his finger down the old entries until he found what he wanted: a debt the Marsh family had owed the workshop since before the midsummer fire.

Edwin Marsh sat at the table and spread all ten fingers flat on the wood to stop them shaking. "I'll testify," he said.

On the windowsill, the Sunstone lens caught the afternoon light. It was still cracked down the middle, just as it had been since the fire. Outside, the roses in Orla's window box had died in the first frost, their heads black and bowed. Orla looked at them, and then at the captain, and nodded.
