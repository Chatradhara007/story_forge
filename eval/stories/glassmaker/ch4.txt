Three years after the fire, the workshop on Tallow Street was open again.

Orla was thirty-seven now, with grey coming in at her temples. She signed the new lease with her right hand, as she always had, and hung the keys on a nail by the door.

Rose came on the first morning with a basket of rosemary. "For luck," said Rose, her cousin, setting it on the bench. Then she unwrapped the other thing she had brought: the pieces of the Sunstone lens, which she had kept in a box for three years. She laid them out, spoke the old binding words over them, and the golden fragments crept together and sealed until the lens was whole and seamless again, without a line to show where it had broken.

Edwin was there too. He no longer called himself Rook. He had dyed his red hair black so the watch would not recognise him in the street, and he had grown tall. Edwin, now twenty-two, had come back to work the furnace, which he did as quickly as ever.

Through the window they could see the canal. Tom Fenner poled his ferry past the workshop, whistling, and raised a hand to them as he went by.

The neighbours still talked about the fire. Everyone agreed it had started at dusk, when the lamps were first being lit, and that it had been set on purpose.
