Chapter Five: The Convent of Saint Ysolde

On the long walk north Aurel kept listening for the hoofbeats of Ember, the black stallion he had left behind in Vessa. It took Aurel and Pell three weeks on foot to reach the convent of Saint Ysolde, which stood on a bare hill above the sheep country of the north, its walls the same grey as the sky.

The sister who opened the gate did not recognise him. The second sister did, and dropped her basket of eggs.

The third was his own sister, Mira.

She was four years younger than him and had always been the quiet one, the one who read in the window seat while Aurel learned swordplay in the yard. Their mother had gone north to the convent the winter King Oren died, and Mira had followed her there at twenty and taken the healer's grey, and Aurel, to his shame, had visited perhaps four times in all those years. Now she stood in the gateway in her stained apron and looked at his beard, and his rope-burned hands, and the boy with the sack behind him.

"Well," Mira said. "You look terrible."

Then she put her arms around him and held on for a long time.

Queen Liesl, mother of the king, was seventy-one years old and nearly blind, and she knew him by his footsteps before he had crossed her cell.

"Aurel," she said. "Sit where I can feel you."

He sat on the floor beside her chair, as he had when he was a boy, and she put her thin hand on his head.

"Corvin writes to me," she said. "Every month. Very kind letters. He tells me the boy is well and the queen is well and the kingdom is recovering. He has always been a good letter writer, your cousin. He was a good letter writer at seventeen." She paused. "He does not mention the bark."

"You know about that?"

"Every pilgrim who climbs this hill knows about that." Her fingers moved in his hair. "Why are you here, my son?"

"I don't know how to come back," he said. It came out of him before he could stop it, the thing he had not said aloud in two years, not even to Pell. "I had everything. I was the king who never lost. And I threw it into the sea, all of it, and now I pole a ferry, and my son is eight years old and being raised by the man who took my crown, and I don't know how to come back."

Liesl was quiet for a while.

"Your father lost a war," she said.

Aurel lifted his head. "No, he didn't."

"He did. You were too young to know, and afterward we made sure nobody told you. When you were two years old, Oren marched on the salt coast with every soldier in Vessmark, and the Venn met him at the river Kell and broke him in a single afternoon. He came home with a third of his army and a fever he never fully shook. The treasury was empty. The harvest failed the next year. That was the famine of his twelfth year, the one you've heard about all your life." Her blind eyes looked somewhere past him. "The granaries at Hollin were not built by a victorious king, Aurel. They were built by a beaten one. He went to every village in the Dun valley himself, on foot, and promised them he would never let them starve again. It took him six years to earn back what he had lost in an afternoon. He did it one village at a time."

"Nobody ever told me."

"No," said Liesl. "We gave you a father who never lost, because we thought it would make you strong. I think now it only made you careless." She patted his head. "Mira. The chest."

Mira brought a long chest from under the narrow bed and opened it. Inside, wrapped in oiled cloth, was a sword: plain, old, its leather grip worn smooth and dark by a hand that was not Aurel's.

"Your father's," said Liesl. "Not his court sword. That one Corvin has, I expect, along with the ring. This is the one he carried to the river Kell and carried home again. He called it Quiet, because he said that after the Kell it never had anything to boast about."

Aurel took the sword. It was lighter than he expected.

"I'm not going to win my crown back with a sword," he said slowly.

"No," said his mother. "I don't imagine you will. Your father didn't."

That night, in the convent kitchen, Mira bound up a burn on Pell's forearm from the cooking fire and asked her brother what he meant to do.

"There's a woman named Saska Venn," Aurel said, "waiting in a tavern in Dunmouth, who has two ships and no reason to trust me. And there's a valley full of people eating bark." He turned his father's sword over in his scarred hands. "I think I'll start with the valley."

"And Corvin?"

"Corvin comes later. If I go to Corvin first, I'm just a man who wants his chair back."

Mira looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face changed.

"I'll come with you," she said. "You'll need a healer. Half of that valley has the flux by now."

"You've taken vows."

"I've taken vows to heal the sick," said Mira, "and they're not up here."
