Chapter Four: The Ferryman

Two years later, the ferry at Dun Crossing was run by a bearded man called Rell.

He was not a good ferryman at first. He capsized the flat-boat twice in his first month and lost a farmer's pig in the second capsizing, and the farmer chased him the length of the bank with a pitchfork. But he learned. He learned to read the river's mood by the colour of the water. He learned to pole against the current with his whole back and not just his arms. He learned what a day's work was worth, which was a copper and a half, and what a loaf cost, which was the same, and he learned to be grateful for it.

He was thirty-five years old. There was grey coming into his beard, and his left hand, with its old pale scar, was rough with rope burns. Nobody who crossed the river looked at him twice. That was the strangest part. For thirty-three years every eye in every room had turned to him, and now he was furniture.

Pell, seventeen now, broad in the shoulders from hauling the ferry chain, kept the accounts in the hut by the landing and did the cooking, which had improved. In the evenings they sat on the bank and Pell read aloud from the broadsheets that came down the river road, because Aurel could not yet bear to read them himself.

"The regent's raised the salt tax again," Pell read one evening. "Third time this year. And there's a new tax on ferries."

"On ferries," said Aurel.

"On us." Pell turned the sheet. "And there's this. 'Prince Tam, now eight years of age, has been received into the household of the Lord Regent at Harrowmere, to be raised as befits his station.'"

Aurel sat very still.

"Harrowmere," he said. "Not Vessa. Corvin's moved him out of the castle. Away from his mother."

"It doesn't say that."

"It doesn't need to." He took the broadsheet and read it himself, for the first time in two years, and then he folded it carefully and put it inside his shirt.

The next morning, a woman came down to the landing alone and asked to be taken across.

She was tall and weathered, with a sailor's rolling walk and close-cropped iron-grey hair, and she carried a sea-chest on her shoulder as though it weighed nothing. Halfway across the river, with the current pulling at the pole, she said, conversationally, "You've got a scar on your left hand, ferryman. Boarding hook, by the look of it."

Aurel did not answer.

"I've seen that scar before," she said. "I saw the hook go in. It was mine. I was trying to pull you off the rail of the Salt Hawk at Greywater, and you cut the rope with your other hand and kept coming." She smiled, and it was not entirely unfriendly. "Saska Venn. Admiral, once. Smuggler, now. Your Majesty."

Aurel kept poling. "I'm not anyone's majesty."

"No," Saska agreed. "And I'm not anyone's admiral. The Venn council threw me out after Greywater. Then your fleet sank at the Kettle Rocks, and they threw me a feast, because they said my ghost had cursed it." She laughed, a short hard bark. "I didn't curse anything. I just watched. It's a terrible thing, watching all nine hundred and forty ships go down, every last one of them. Even when they're coming to kill you."

They reached the far bank. Saska put her sea-chest on the landing and did not pick it up again.

"Here is what I know," she said. "The isles had a good harvest this year. Your Vessmark had another bad one. Your cousin Corvin is buying Venn grain through three different merchants, at three times the fair price, and he is selling it in Vessa at six times the price, and he is keeping the difference. The people in the Dun valley are eating bark again." She looked at him levelly. "I can carry grain. I have two ships and a crew who are bored of smuggling wine. What I don't have is a reason to trust a man who tried to burn my islands."

"Neither do I," said Aurel.

Saska Venn considered this, and then she laughed again, longer this time.

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing a king of Vessmark has ever said to me." She hoisted the chest. "I'll be at the Lamb and Anchor in Dunmouth for a week. If you decide you're still somebody, come and find me."

That night Aurel did not sleep. He turned his father's signet ring on his finger, the crowned salt-hawk worn smooth, while Pell rubbed down Ember behind the ferry hut. He sat on the bank of the river Dun with the folded broadsheet in his lap and watched the water go by until the sky turned grey.

In the morning he said to Pell, "I need to see my mother."
