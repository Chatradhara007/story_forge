Chapter Seven: The Long Road Back

Four months later, the people of the Dun valley had stopped eating bark.

It had not happened quickly, and it had not happened because of a king. It had happened because a smuggler with two ships agreed, after a long and suspicious evening at the Lamb and Anchor in Dunmouth, to carry Venn grain up the river at the fair price and not a copper more, and to be paid when the harvest came in rather than before. Saska Venn had driven the hardest bargain Aurel had ever lost, and he had lost it gladly.

"I'm not doing this for you," she told him, the night the first barge came up to Dun Crossing low in the water with sacks of island wheat. "I'm doing it because my grandmother starved the winter after the Kell war. Your father's army burned our coastal fields on the way to the river. We won the battle and went hungry anyway."

"He lost that war," said Aurel. "I only found out this year. They never told me."

Saska looked at him sideways. "Huh," she said. And after a moment: "Nobody ever told me about the fields, either. I found that out from my grandmother's grave."

The grain went up the valley on carts and on mules and, where the roads had washed away, on the backs of the men who had once been Aurel's soldiers and were now his neighbours. He went with it. He walked from village to village beside the carts, as his father had done before him, and in every village he did the same thing. He stood in the square, said his true name, and told them the truth.

"I am Aurel, who was your king. I sold the grain your fathers stored. I sank the fleet. Your sons drowned at the Kettle Rocks because of me. I am not here to ask for anything. I am here with wheat, and with my elder sister, who is a healer, and with a boy named Pell who keeps better accounts than my old treasurer. Bring your sick to my sister. Bring your hungry to the carts."

In the first village they threw stones at him, and one of them opened his forehead, and Mira stitched it in the back of the cart while he swore. In the second village they would not speak to him at all. In the third, an old woman spat at his feet, and then took a sack of wheat, and then came back an hour later and spat at his feet again, and then asked whether her grandson could ride with the carts to the next village and help unload.

By the seventh village, they were waiting for him at the bridge.

Mira worked through the flux season with her sleeves rolled to the elbow. Pell, seventeen and suddenly a quartermaster, kept the ledger of every sack and every copper and every promise, and read it aloud in each square so that nobody could say they had been cheated. Aurel carried sacks, dug latrines, mended a mill wheel with his own hands, and slept in barns. His forehead healed into a second scar to match the one on his right hand. He turned thirty-eight that winter, in a hayloft above a mill. He did not look like a king. He had stopped trying to.

At the end of the fourth month, on a wet night at the ford of Hollin, a single rider came down the south road on a tired horse, and the rider was Bram Holloway.

He was sixty-two now, and he got down from the saddle very slowly. He brought a letter from Queen Liesl, written in her own small, careful hand; she wrote that she had read every one of her son's letters twice by candlelight. Aurel ran to him through the mud.

"You stupid old man. You rode from Vessa? Alone?"

"Someone had to tell you." Bram held on to his shoulder to steady himself. "Corvin knows where you are. Half of Vessa knows. They're singing songs about the ferryman king in the lower city, did you know that? Bad ones. The rhymes are terrible." He coughed. "The regent has stopped buying Venn grain. He says the crisis is over. It isn't. He has the last of it locked up in the cellars at Harrowmere, and he's selling it off a sack at a time to the guild masters who sit on his council."

"Thessaly?"

"Well. Angry. She hasn't spoken to Corvin since he took the boy to Harrowmere. She reads the regent's own accounts aloud in the great hall every month, and he lets her, because he doesn't know how to stop her." The old swordmaster's mouth twitched. "Tam asks for you. Every week. Corvin has told him you're dead. Tam doesn't believe it. Your son is as stubborn as you were."

Aurel stood in the rain for a long moment.

"The council meets at the spring equinox," Bram went on. "All of them, in the long chamber. The regent wants to make the regency permanent: Lord Protector until Tam is twenty-five. If it passes, the boy will be a grown man before he ever sits in his own chair, and by then he'll be whatever Corvin has made him."

"Then I'll be there."

"With what? An army?"

Aurel looked back up the valley, at the lights of the villages along the river, and the barges at the ford, and Saska Venn arguing with a miller on the landing, and Mira asleep sitting up in the back of a cart with her apron still on, and Pell writing in the ledger by lantern light.

"No," he said. "Not an army."

He took his father's sword Quiet from the cart and buckled it on for the first time. It sat easily at his hip, as though it had been waiting.
