Chapter Eight: The Crown Without the King

The council met at the spring equinox, in the long chamber under the painted ceiling, at the hour of the morning bell. Duke Corvin sat in the king's chair with the signet ring of the house of Oren on his finger, a little too big for him still, and the eleven councillors sat around the table, and the paper that would make the regency permanent lay in front of them, waiting for wax.

They had reached the vote when they heard the noise from the lower city.

It was not the roar of a riot. Aurel had heard that roar once, and he would never forget it. This was a different sound: thousands of feet, walking, and under it, very faintly, singing. The rhymes were terrible.

They had come up the river road from the Dun valley, many of them widows of the spring storm at the Kettle Rocks that took the fleet, on carts and on foot and on Saska Venn's two barges, which had sailed brazenly into the royal harbour under the flag of the Venn isles: millers and farmers, mothers and grandfathers, the old woman who had spat at Aurel's feet twice, and every village between Hollin and Dun Crossing. They carried no weapons. They carried sacks: the empty grain sacks from the barges, one for every family that had been fed through the winter, and they laid them down in the great square of Vessa in a grey heap taller than a man.

At the front of them, in a ferryman's coat, with two scars and his father's plain old sword, walked the king who had lost everything.

The guards at the castle gate did not stop him. Some of them had served at Greywater. One of them had a brother in the Dun valley. They stood aside, and Aurel walked through his own gate and up his own stair, with Bram on one side and Mira on the other, and Pell and Saska behind, and he opened the door of the long chamber without knocking.

Corvin rose. It was the first time he had stood for his cousin since the morning he took the chair.

"You're supposed to be dead," Corvin said.

"So I've heard."

For a long time nobody spoke. Through the windows came the sound of the crowd in the square, waiting.

"I didn't do it for the chair," Corvin said at last, very low. "You have to believe that. I did it because you were going to starve the whole kingdom for a fleet and a toast. And then I sat in this chair, and the harvest failed again, and I was afraid. I was so afraid, cousin. Every night I thought, this is how it felt for him, and I locked the grain away so that I would never be the one to run out." He looked down at the ring on his finger. "I became exactly the thing I took the crown away from."

"I know," said Aurel. "I've been there."

He did not draw his sword. He did not ask for the chair. He walked to the window and threw it open, so that the councillors could see the square below and the grey mountain of empty sacks.

"There are the people of the Dun valley," he said. "They are not here for me. They are here because the regent has a cellar full of grain at Harrowmere, and they want it opened. Vote on that. Then, if you like, vote on me."

They voted on the cellar first. It was not close.

Afterward, when the councillors had gone out to the square, Corvin took the signet ring off his finger and held it out across the table. Aurel looked at it for a long while before he took it.

"They'll want you back," Corvin said. "You know they will. They're already calling you the ferryman king."

"Then they'll have me on conditions," said Aurel. "The granaries full and the gates open. A council that can say no to me, and a law that says they must, if I ever again try to sell bread to buy ships. And a regent's chair kept beside mine, for the next time I'm a fool." He looked at his cousin. "I'd like you to sit in it. I think you'll know what it's for."

Corvin's face broke. He did not answer, but he did not refuse.

The queen was waiting at the head of the great stair. She had heard the singing from the nursery window. Thessaly looked at her husband's beard, and his two scars, and the ferryman's coat, and the ring back on his finger, and she did not run to him. She folded her arms.

"Well?" she said. "What are you, when you're not the king?"

Aurel thought about it honestly, the way she had taught him to think about the figures in the great ledger when he was nineteen.

"A man who knows how much a loaf costs," he said.

Thessaly's dark brown eyes filled, and then she laughed, and then she did run to him.

Tam came down the stair behind his mother, taller than Aurel remembered, all knees and elbows, and stopped three steps from the bottom and stared.

"I told them you weren't dead," Tam said.

"You were right."

"Did you make the promise again? About nobody being hungry?"

"I'm making it now," said Aurel, and knelt on the stone in front of his son, so that their eyes were level. "And this time I'll show you where the granaries are, so that if I ever forget, you can remind me."

That evening Pell led an old grey stallion out of the royal stables into the spring dusk. Ember had grown white around the muzzle since Aurel had last seen him, and he was too old now to carry a king into battle. He whickered and pushed his nose against Aurel's chest and would not let him go.

In the great hall, there was a feast: a small one, bread and cheese and Venn wine, which Saska Venn swore was legally imported. Bram Holloway sat at the end of the lower table, the way he always had. When Aurel raised his cup, the old swordmaster looked at it for a long moment.

Then, for the first time since the night of Greywater, Bram turned his own cup the right way up, and filled it, and drank.
