Chapter Three: The Council of Knives

The council met at the hour of the morning bell, the next day, in the long chamber under the painted ceiling where Aurel's grandfather had signed the first salt treaty. Aurel walked in without being announced. Eleven heads turned toward him, and not one of them rose.

Duke Corvin sat in the king's chair.

"Cousin," said Corvin. He did not get up either. "Sit down. Please."

There was no other empty chair. Aurel understood that this had been arranged, and that the arranging had taken time, and that it had probably begun long before the storm at the Kettle Rocks. He remained standing.

"Get out of my chair, Corvin."

"It isn't yours this morning," said the Lord Treasurer, very quietly. "Forgive me, Majesty. The council has voted."

They laid it out for him like a ledger. The grain reserve, sold. The fleet, lost. Nine hundred ships and their crews at the bottom of the sea, and nothing to show for it but sixty battered hulls and widows in every harbour town from Vessa to Harrowmere. The riots in the lower city. Seven dead in the market square. The southern merchants refusing credit. The guild masters threatening to close the city gates against the king's own soldiers.

"We are not asking for your death," said Corvin, and Aurel believed him, which was somehow worse. "We are asking for your abdication. Duke Corvin will serve as regent until Prince Tam comes of age. The queen and the prince will stay here, under my protection, and lack nothing. You will retire to Harrowmere, to the castle you gave me, and live there in comfort."

"As a prisoner."

"As a guest."

Aurel looked around the table. These were the men who had cheered him at Greywater. The Lord Treasurer would not meet his eyes. Old Marshal Dace stared at the painted ceiling. Only Corvin looked at him directly, and in Corvin's face Aurel saw something he had never seen there before. It was not hatred. It was pity.

"You toasted me," Aurel said. "Two years ago. You said I had never lost."

"I know," said Corvin. "I was wrong. So were you."

They had the abdication already written. Aurel read it twice. Then he took the signet ring of his father, the crowned salt-hawk of the house of Oren, off his finger, set it on the table, and pushed it across the wood toward his cousin.

He did not sign the paper. He walked out.

He went to the nursery first. Thessaly was there, already dressed, as though she had known. Tam was asleep.

"Come with me," Aurel said.

"Where?" she asked. Not angry; simply asking. "To Harrowmere, to be Corvin's guests? Or into the woods, with a six-year-old, and no money, and no name? Aurel, if I take him out of this castle, Corvin's council will call it a kidnapping, and the next regent will not be as gentle as your cousin." She took his scarred hand in both of hers. "He is safest here. You know it."

"Then I'll stay."

"If you stay, you go to Harrowmere in chains, and you never come back." Her grey-green eyes were steady and wet. "Go. Find out what you are when you're not the king. And when you know, come back and tell me."

He kissed his sleeping son without waking him.

Bram Holloway was waiting at the foot of the servants' stair with a cloak, a purse, and a sword that was not a king's sword: plain steel, a soldier's blade. "The water gate," said the old man. "The guards there are mine, still. You have until the noon bell."

"Come with me."

"I'm sixty-six years old," said Bram. "I'd slow you down. And someone has to stay and keep an eye on the boy." He pushed the cloak into the king's arms. "Go."

At the water gate, Aurel found that he was not alone. Pell, the stable boy, was crouched by the iron grille with a sack over his shoulder.

"Ember's too big for the water gate," Pell said, before the king could speak. "I'm sorry. I had to leave him. But I can carry things, and I can cook, sort of, and I know the river roads." He was fifteen and shaking. "You can't go out there by yourself. You've never even lit a fire."

That was true. Aurel had won a war, and he had never lit a fire.

"All right," he said.

They went out through the water gate as the noon bell began to ring, a king without a ring and a boy without a horse, and the river Dun carried them south and away from Vessa in the bottom of a fishing boat that smelled of eels.

Behind them, in the long chamber, Duke Corvin slid the signet ring onto his own finger. It was a little too big for him. He wore it anyway.
