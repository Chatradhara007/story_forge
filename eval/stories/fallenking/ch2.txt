Chapter Two: The Lean Year

The rain began in the spring of the second year after Greywater, and it did not stop.

It fell on the wheat fields of the Dun valley until the seed rotted in the ground. It fell on the orchards of Hollin until the blossom turned brown and dropped. By midsummer the farmers were standing in their fields up to their ankles in water, staring at nothing, and by autumn the price of a loaf in the markets of Vessa had risen sevenfold.

King Aurel was thirty-three years old, and for the first time in his life he did not know what to do.

"Open the granaries," the guild masters begged him in the great hall.

"The granaries are empty," said the Lord Treasurer, who would not meet the king's eyes. "The reserve was sold to the southern merchants two years ago, Your Majesty. To pay for the fleet."

Nobody in the hall said the word that everyone was thinking. They did not need to.

The fleet itself was finished. Nine hundred and forty ships, not quite the thousand Aurel had promised, rode at anchor in the harbour and along the coast. In the second week of the autumn storms the king ordered them to sail for the Venn isles. If he could take the islands before winter, he reasoned, the Venn granaries would feed Vessmark, and the war and the hunger would end together.

Admiral Saska Venn, who had lost Greywater and spent two years being hated for it by her own people, watched from the cliffs of Venn as the storm caught the Vessmark fleet at the Kettle Rocks. Of nine hundred and forty ships, sixty came home.

The news reached Vessa on a grey morning. Aurel read the dispatch three times standing in his study, and then he sat down on the floor with his back against the desk, because his legs would no longer hold him.

That was where Thessaly found him.

"How many?" she asked.

"Sixty came back."

She knelt beside him. Through the window they could hear a noise from the lower city, a noise Aurel had never heard before: not cheering, not bells, but a low, steady roar, like the sea in a cave.

"They're at the gates of the old granary," Thessaly said. "There's nothing inside, and they know it, and they're breaking it open anyway. Aurel, listen to me. You have to go down there. Not with soldiers. With bread, if you can find any, and with the truth if you can't."

"I don't have any bread."

"Then go with the truth."

He did not go. He sent Duke Corvin instead, with two hundred men of the guard, because Corvin had held the left wing at Greywater and could hold a crowd. Corvin came back at dusk with blood on his sleeve and seven dead in the market square, and he stood in the king's study and said quietly, "It cannot go on like this, cousin."

"I know."

"No," said Corvin. "I don't think you do."

In the nursery that night, Tam, six years old now, asked his father why the people were angry.

Aurel sat on the edge of the little bed and turned the signet ring on his finger. The scar on the back of his left hand ached in the cold the way it always did now.

"Because I made a promise to them," he said at last, "and I broke it."

"Which promise?"

"That they would never be hungry."

Tam thought about this with the enormous seriousness of a six-year-old. "Can you make it again?"

Aurel had no answer. He kissed his son on the forehead and went out, and in the corridor he found his old swordmaster sitting on a bench, waiting for him as he had waited for him outside a hundred rooms.

"Bram," the king said. "Say it."

"I don't need to say it."

"Say it anyway. You've earned it."

Bram Holloway looked at him for a long moment. "I told you it was the twelfth year," he said. And then, more gently: "Corvin has called the council for tomorrow morning. All of them. He didn't send for you."

Aurel stood very still. "He can't call the council without the king."

"He can," said Bram, "if the council has already decided it doesn't have one."

In the stables, Pell was rubbing down Ember for the night. The grey stallion was restless; he could smell the smoke from the lower city. Pell talked to him softly, the way he always did. The boy was fifteen now, tall and all elbows, and he had stopped being afraid of the king years ago. He had never imagined being afraid for him.

Outside, the rain kept falling on Vessa.
