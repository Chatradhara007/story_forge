Chapter Six: The Boy King

Fourteen years before the fall, on the coldest morning of the year, King Oren died of the fever he had carried home from the river Kell, and his son Aurel, nineteen years old, was crowned before the body was cold.

There was no time for anything else. The Venn were raiding the salt coast again, the lords of the north were muttering, and a kingdom without a king for even a week was a kingdom with a dozen kings by the end of it. So they put the crown on Aurel's head in the cold chapel with the snow blowing in under the door, and Bram Holloway, fifty-two years old and already grey at the temples, knelt first and kissed the boy's hand and swore to him.

Aurel had no scar on his left hand then. He had never been in a battle. He had never been anywhere much at all.

His cousin Corvin knelt second. Corvin was seventeen, thin and red-haired and ferociously loyal, and when he rose he did not let go of Aurel's hand.

"Whatever happens," Corvin said, "I am yours."

"I know," said Aurel.

That night, alone in his father's study with the snow still falling, the new king opened the great ledger of the kingdom for the first time and stared at the columns of figures until they swam. He did not understand most of them. He understood the largest one well enough: the grain stored at Hollin, enough to feed the whole of Vessmark through two bad winters. His father had written beside it, in his own cramped hand, a single line: Never again.

Aurel did not know what it meant. He meant to ask, and never did.

Bram found him there at midnight.

"You should sleep," said the swordmaster.

"I don't know how to be a king," said Aurel. "I don't know what any of this is for."

"Nobody knows that at nineteen." Bram sat down across the desk. "I'll tell you the only thing your father ever told me about it. He said a king is a man who keeps the granaries full and the gates open. Everything else is decoration."

Aurel wrote it on a scrap of paper and tucked it into the ledger, and over the years the scrap of paper was lost, and he forgot.

In the spring of that first year a shipwright from the harbour came to the castle to bid for the repair of the royal barge, and he brought his daughter to carry his drawings. Her name was Thessaly. She was eighteen, with grey-green eyes and pitch on her fingers, and she spent the whole audience correcting her father's figures under her breath. Aurel, who had not understood a single figure in the great ledger, asked her to explain them to him.

She explained them for three hours. By the end of the summer he had asked her to marry him, and by the end of the next summer she had said yes, and the lords of the north muttered about a shipwright's daughter on the throne until the first time she read their own accounts aloud to them in open court.

In those first years Corvin rode everywhere at Aurel's side. They put down the northern lords together. They rebuilt the harbour wall together. One night, drunk on southern wine on the battlements of Vessa, twenty years old and immortal, Corvin swore that he would never want anything that was Aurel's.

"Not your crown, not your queen, not your horse," Corvin said, and laughed, because Aurel had just been given a grey colt from the royal stud and would let nobody else ride it. "Especially not your horse."

The colt's name was Ember. He threw Aurel eleven times before he would let him stay on.

Years afterward, poling a ferry across the river Dun, Aurel would try to remember the exact moment the boy king became the king who never lost: the moment he stopped asking Thessaly to explain the figures, and stopped hearing what Bram meant, and started believing his own toasts. He never found it. There was no single moment. It happened the way a riverbank goes, a handful of earth at a time, until one spring the whole bank is in the water and you cannot remember where it used to stand.
