Chapter One: The Salt Crown

The bells of Vessa rang for three days after Greywater, and on the third night King Aurel stood on the high table of his own hall and let the city love him.

He was thirty-one years old and he had never lost anything that mattered. The Venn fleet lay broken on the shoals of Greywater bay, its admiral fled, and the salt coast that his father had spent a lifetime begging for now belonged to Vessmark without a single coin paid. The scar was still fresh across the back of his left hand, a pale line where a Venn boarding hook had opened him to the bone. He held that hand up now, with the cup in it, so that the whole hall could see it.

"To Greywater," he said.

"To Greywater," roared four hundred voices, and the rafters shook.

At his side sat Queen Thessaly in green, her grey-green eyes watching the room the way a shipwright's daughter watches a hull in the water, looking for the place where it will leak. Their son Tam, four years old and long past his bedtime, slept against her shoulder with his fist wrapped in her sleeve.

"They'll remember this night," Aurel said to her, low.

"They'll remember what you do with it," said Thessaly.

He laughed and kissed her hand, because he did not yet understand that she was not joking.

Below the high table, at the right hand of the king, sat Duke Corvin, Aurel's cousin, two years younger than him and the best friend he had ever had. Corvin's red hair caught the torchlight. He had commanded the left wing at Greywater and had held it when the Venn galleys broke through, and Aurel had already given him the castle of Harrowmere as a reward. Corvin rose and lifted his own cup.

"To the King," Corvin said, "who has never lost."

It was a fine toast. Aurel would remember it later and wonder whether it had been a blessing or a curse.

Only one man in the hall did not drink. Bram Holloway, sixty-four years old, the swordmaster who had taught Aurel to hold a blade when he was seven, sat at the end of the lower table with his cup turned upside down. Bram had fought for Aurel's father, King Oren, and he had a way of looking at a victory as though it were a debt that had not yet come due.

When the hall had emptied and the servants were sweeping up the bones, Aurel found the old man on the battlements.

"You didn't drink," Aurel said.

"I'm too old to drink to things that haven't finished happening," said Bram.

"Greywater is finished. The Venn are finished."

"The Venn are angry and alive. That is a different thing from finished." Bram leaned on the wall and looked down at the harbour, where the captured Venn galleys rocked against the quay with Vessmark banners nailed to their masts. "What will you do now?"

Aurel had been waiting all night for someone to ask him. "I will finish it," he said. "A thousand ships. We build them over two summers, we sail them to the Venn isles, and we end the war for a hundred years. My father dreamed of the salt coast. I will give my son the whole sea."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. "And how does a king pay for a thousand ships?"

"With the grain reserve." Aurel said it lightly, the way a man mentions the weather. "The granaries at Hollin are full to the roof. Three good harvests in a row. We sell the surplus to the southern merchants, and the silver buys timber and pitch and shipwrights."

"The grain reserve is not surplus," Bram said. "Your father filled it after the famine of his twelfth year. It is the thing that stands between this city and the next bad harvest."

"There will not be a bad harvest. There hasn't been one in eleven years."

"That," said the old man, "is exactly what every king says in the twelfth year."

Aurel felt the old irritation rise, the one he had felt as a boy when Bram corrected his grip for the hundredth time. He was the king who had won Greywater. He did not need to be corrected.

"Bram," he said, "I love you. But the war has changed, and you have not."

The old swordmaster turned his upside-down cup over in his hands and said nothing more.

Below them, in the royal stables, Aurel's grey stallion Ember stamped and snorted at the noise of the drunken city. Pell, the stable boy, thirteen years old and in love with every horse in Vessmark, sat up with him until dawn.

In the morning Aurel signed the order. The granary doors at Hollin were opened, the grain was loaded onto southern barges, and the first keels of the thousand ships were laid in the yards of Vessa before the month was out. He pressed his father's signet ring into the hot wax himself: the crowned salt-hawk of the house of Oren.

The city cheered when the first ship slid into the water. Aurel stood on the slipway with Tam on his shoulders and pointed out to sea.

"All of that," he told his son, "will be yours."

It was the highest point of his life. He did not know it, because nobody ever does.
