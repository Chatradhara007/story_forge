Chapter Five

The inspection came the following Monday, a week after the bus went into the river. Three people in grey suits walked through the department with clipboards and asked Tom Okafor why the desk had a box on it.

"It's for my arm," said Tom, and lifted the patient in bed two up the bed with both arms, the cast and all, to show them he was perfectly fit for work.

The inspectors wrote something down. In bed four, Walter Alcott raised a hand and waved at them over his newspaper, and asked loudly whether they had come about the food.

Priya showed them round. She had slept, for once, and caught sight of herself in the glass of the resuscitation room: a small woman with tired brown eyes and a scar through one eyebrow. She looked, she thought, like someone who would close a department.

At the end of the visit the lead inspector asked what time it was. Priya flipped open her father's brass pocket watch, the one she had carried every shift for six years, and told him: twenty past eleven.

Marguerite came down in the afternoon. She had had her grey hair dyed a dark chestnut for the hospital's charity dinner and was furious that everyone had noticed.

"Not one word," she said to Tom.

"I wasn't going to say anything," said Tom. "It's very nice."

Her younger sister Marguerite, Priya thought, had always been the vain one; she watched her stalk back to the lift and felt, for the first time that week, like laughing.

Felix ran the night shift alone that evening, for the first time. Nobody died. In the morning he looked ten years older and very pleased.
