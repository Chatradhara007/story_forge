Chapter Two

Tuesday was worse. A bus had gone into the river at the bottom of Calder Street, and by ten o'clock the department was a corridor of wet coats and shouting.

Tom Okafor slipped on the flooded floor of the ambulance bay and went down hard on his left arm. Felix heard the wrist go from across the room.

"It's broken," Tom said, very calmly, looking at the angle of it. "Well. That's annoying."

By midnight his left forearm was in a plaster cast up to the elbow, and the orthopaedic registrar had told him six weeks, no lifting, and no arguing. Tom argued anyway. Priya put him on the desk, where he could boss people around one-handed.

Felix stitched his first scalp wound and only had to redo it once. Priya watched him, arms folded, and said nothing, which he correctly took as praise.

Walter Alcott was still in bed four, waiting for a bed upstairs that did not exist. He had made friends with the man in bed five and was teaching him a card game that seemed to have no rules.

"You've got the hands of a surgeon," Walter told Felix, loudly, because he could not hear himself. "Shame about the hair."

Near dawn Marguerite came down from the fourth floor with coffee for everyone and a list of complaints from the night manager. She stood in the doorway in her grey cardigan, sixty years old and unimpressed by the river, the bus and the flood.

"Leo's asleep," she told her sister. "Go home after this. You look like a ghost."

Priya did not go home.
