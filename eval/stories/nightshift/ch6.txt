Chapter Six

Six weeks later, Tom Okafor had his cast cut off in the plaster room and spent the afternoon carrying boxes of saline to prove that he could. His wrist was thin and pale and ached in the cold, and he would not hear a word about it.

The department had not been closed. The inspectors' report said it was overcrowded, underfunded and well run, and Marguerite had it framed.

Walter Alcott's daughter came in on a quiet afternoon to collect her father's glasses and his pack of cards. She sat with Priya in the relatives' room and asked whether he had been frightened.

"No," said Priya. "He was tired. And he was winning."

That night, as the shift began, Priya walked down Fenwick Street to St Brennan's in the rain, the way she had for six years. Felix was already there. At twenty-four he was the youngest resident the department had ever had, and he no longer looked terrified, only tired, like everyone else.

He took out the brass pocket watch to time a child's breathing, then caught her looking.

"I can give it back," he said.

"Keep it," said Priya. "My father would have liked you."

Leo came in at midnight with Marguerite, who had been to the cinema with him and had brought sandwiches for the whole shift. They sat at the desk with Tom and argued about the film.

People still talked about the night of the blackout, the Saturday the lights went out on Calder Street. Priya let them get the details wrong. It was a better story that way.
